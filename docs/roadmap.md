**1. Set up and ship.** A working Linux environment, notebooks, and version
control. Your first project pushed to GitHub in week one, because the habit
matters more than the code in it.

**2. Read the maths.** Linear algebra and probability treated as the language
models are written in. Vectors, matrices, distributions, uncertainty. Enough to
read a formula and know what it is doing, not enough to sit an exam.

**3. Move data with code.** NumPy and pandas until reshaping a dataset is
faster than opening a spreadsheet. Filtering, grouping, joining, aggregating.

**4. Get it and clean it.** Pull data from files, APIs, SQL and NoSQL
databases. Then handle what is missing, duplicated, mistyped or quietly wrong,
which is most of it.

**5. Explain it, then predict.** Visualisation and statistics that hold up to
questioning. Then your first models: linear and tree based methods for when you
have labels, clustering and dimensionality reduction for when you do not.

**1. Build a network.** Feed forward networks layer by layer, from a single
unit upward, until nothing inside one is a black box to you.

**2. Make training work.** Loss functions, optimisers, learning rates,
initialisation and batching. The settings that decide whether a model converges
at all, and what to change when it does not.

**3. Diagnose failure.** Error analysis and regularisation. Given a model that
underperforms, work out whether the problem is the data, the capacity, the
training, or the question, and fix the right one.

**4. Teach it to see.** Convolutions and pooling, deep vision architectures,
augmentation, transfer learning and object detection. A vision problem solved
end to end.

**5. Teach it to remember.** Recurrent networks and sequences, built fluently
in Keras and TensorFlow rather than copied from a tutorial.

**1. Forecast what comes next.** Time series, where the order of the data is
the whole problem and the usual shuffling tricks quietly destroy the answer.

**2. Turn language into numbers.** NLP fundamentals and embeddings. How text
becomes something a model can work with, and what gets lost on the way.

**3. Open up the chatbot.** Transformers and attention. What a large language
model is actually doing when it answers, and what that means for when to trust
it.

**4. Ground it in your own data.** Retrieval augmented generation, so a system
answers from documents you control instead of from what it happens to have
memorised.

**5. Let it act.** Agents, tool use and MCP. Systems that do things in the
world rather than only describe them, and the judgment about when that is a
good idea.

## The finish

The programme ends with a portfolio project of your own choosing, built to a
standard you would be comfortable defending in an interview. Not a tutorial
followed to completion. Something you decided to make.

By then you will have three trimesters of projects behind it, which is the
difference between a candidate who says they learned machine learning and one
who can open a repository and show it.

## How material appears

Each module opens on this site the week the cohort reaches it. That is
deliberate. The order is what makes the nine months work, and having everything
visible at once encourages skipping ahead to the interesting-sounding part
before the foundation is there.

The roadmap stays complete, so you always know what is coming.
