    Project 4: Beat the baseline¶

    Due 2 December Classic models Teams of 2 or 3 Five datasets

    Project 3 banned models. This one is built around them. But a model is only worth something next to the simplest guess you could have made without one, so that is where you start, and that is the number you have to beat.

    What you are doing¶

    Five teams, five datasets, all from scikit-learn. Two teams predict a number. Three teams predict a class. Everybody follows the same pipeline, and everybody answers the same question at the end: how much better than the obvious guess is your model, give or take?

    What everyone must show¶

    Same objectives for every team. What they mean for your data is in your tab.

    Objective
    0 Load the data, cache it, write the data contract, look at what is there
    1 Split off a test set before modelling anything, with a fixed seed (stratified if you predict a class)
    2 Score a baseline, exactly the way you will score your models
    3 Train at least two models: a plain linear one, and one with more to tune (regularised, or a kernel)
    4 Tune with cross-validation on the training set
    5 Score the final model on the test set once, with a bootstrap range, next to the baseline
    6 Find where the model fails, show examples, and explain why
    7 Write it up: the notebook in full, the poster in one page

    The test set is opened once

    Every decision, which features, which model, which hyperparameters, is made without looking at the test set. You score on it once, at the end, and whatever number comes out is the number you report.

    If you score on the test set, change something, and score again, it has become a second validation set, and your result is now an overestimate that nobody can correct.

    These datasets are small, between 442 and 20,640 rows. Holding out a separate validation set as well would leave very little to train on, so you tune with cross-validation instead.

    What you hand in¶

    Two things, from the same work. The notebook is the full record. The poster is the finding, for someone who has thirty seconds.

    • The notebook

      • The data contract at the top
      • Every phase of the pipeline, in order, with the decisions written down in markdown, not just made in code
      • Baseline and models scored the same way, with ranges
      • At least three failure cases, shown and explained
      • Runs top to bottom from cached data, wifi off, fixed seed

    • The poster

      • One page, landscape, exported to PDF
      • The finding as the title, a full sentence
      • One main result with its range and its baseline
      • One figure, labelled so it reads without you there
      • Where it fails, what did not work, one takeaway

    The poster follows this layout. Fill the frame; do not redesign it.

    Hand both in from your team repository:

    project-4/
├── data/raw/        # the dataset saved as CSV, committed
├── notebook.ipynb
└── poster.pdf

    Three of the datasets ship inside scikit-learn. California Housing and German Credit download the first time you load them. Either way, save the table to data/raw/ once and load it from there, so the notebook runs without the internet.

    Same pipeline, different scoreboard¶

    The pipeline does not change between the two kinds of problem. What changes is how you keep score.

    Regression Classification
    You predict a number a class, and the probability behind it
    Baseline the training mean, then a simple rule given in your tab the majority class, then a simple rule given in your tab
    Main metric MAE, in the target's own units precision, recall and F1 on the class you care about
    Always show residuals against predictions the confusion matrix
    The range bootstrap the test set bootstrap the test set

    Regression: predict a number¶

    The question. Income, house age, rooms and location describe every district in California. How well can a linear model turn them into a house price, and where does a straight line stop being enough?

    The data. California Housing, 20,640 districts from the 1990 census, 8 numeric columns. Target: median house value, in units of $100,000.

    from sklearn import datasets
housing = datasets.fetch_california_housing(as_frame=True)
df = housing.frame

    Baseline. First the training mean for every district. Then a one-column rule: split median income into ten bands and predict the average value of the band, from the training data.

    Must do. Beat the income rule on MAE, with a range, and report it in dollars. Then report your error separately for cheap, middle and expensive districts, and say where the model struggles and why.

    Watch out for. The target is capped at 5.0: every district worth more than $500,000 is recorded as exactly $500,000. You will see it as a hard edge in your residual plot, so decide what to do about it and say so. House age is capped too, at 52. A few districts have absurd averages for rooms and occupancy, because only a handful of households live there. Latitude and longitude matter, but not in a straight line.

    If you finish early. Split by geography instead of at random: train on the north of the state and test on the south. How much worse does the model get, and what does that say about using it somewhere new?

    The question. Ten measurements taken at a first visit, one number a year later. With only 442 patients, can a model do meaningfully better than the average, and can you prove it is not luck?

    The data. The diabetes dataset: age, sex, body mass index, blood pressure and six blood serum measurements (s1 to s6) for 442 patients. Target: a measure of disease progression one year later.

    from sklearn import datasets
diabetes = datasets.load_diabetes(as_frame=True, scaled=False)
df = diabetes.frame

    Baseline. First the training mean. Then a one-column rule: split body mass index into four bands and predict the average target of the band.

    Must do. Compare LinearRegression, Ridge and Lasso, choosing alpha with cross-validation. Beat the BMI rule on MAE, with a range, and say honestly whether the ranges of your best model and the baseline overlap. Show which measurements Lasso keeps as alpha grows.

    Watch out for. Load it with scaled=False. The default version was scaled using all 442 patients, test set included, which is a small leak. Scale it yourself, on the training set only. With so few rows a single split can swing your score a lot, so your ranges will be wide, and that is a finding, not a failure. s1 and s2 are strongly related, which is exactly where Ridge and Lasso disagree.

    If you finish early. Repeat the whole split 50 times with different seeds and plot the 50 test scores. How much could one unlucky split have fooled you?

    Classification: predict a class¶

    The question. Thirty measurements of cell nuclei from a scan. A model that misses a malignant tumour is far worse than one that raises a false alarm. How many malignant cases can you catch, and at what price?

    The data. The breast cancer dataset: 569 tumours, 30 numeric measurements. Target: malignant or benign.

    from sklearn import datasets
cancer = datasets.load_breast_cancer(as_frame=True)
df = cancer.frame

    Baseline. Two of them. Always predict the majority class (benign). Then a one-column rule: pick a single measurement and a threshold from the training data, and call everything above it malignant.

    Must do. Beat the one-column rule on recall for malignant tumours, with a range, without letting precision collapse. Choose your probability threshold, defend it in one paragraph, and show the confusion matrix at that threshold.

    Watch out for. In this dataset 0 means malignant and 1 means benign, the opposite of what you would guess. If you do not flip it, your "recall" is measuring the wrong class. The 30 columns are 10 measurements each recorded three ways (mean, standard error, worst), so radius, perimeter and area say almost the same thing. With accuracy around 95 percent for almost any model, accuracy tells you nothing here.

    If you finish early. Use an L1 penalty on logistic regression and find the smallest set of measurements that keeps recall within your range.

    The question. Ten classes instead of two, and pixels instead of columns with names. When does a straight boundary stop being enough, and which digits does every model confuse?

    The data. The digits dataset: 1,797 handwritten digits, each an 8 × 8 image, so 64 pixel columns with values from 0 to 16. Target: the digit, 0 to 9.

    from sklearn import datasets
digits = datasets.load_digits(as_frame=True)
df = digits.frame

    Baseline. Always predicting one digit scores about 10 percent, so that is not much of a bar. The real baseline: average all training images of each digit, and label a new image with the closest average (neighbors.NearestCentroid). Expect it to be right close to 9 times in 10.

    Must do. Beat the average-image rule on macro F1, with a range. Compare a linear model with an SVM using the RBF kernel. Show the 10 × 10 confusion matrix and at least nine misclassified images, and say what they have in common.

    Watch out for. With ten classes, precision, recall and F1 need an average= argument: use "macro" so every digit counts equally, and say so. The images come from a few dozen writers, so a random split puts the same handwriting on both sides, and your score is probably a little optimistic for a new writer. Scale the pixels before the SVM.

    If you finish early. Reshape the 64 weights a linear model learned for each digit back into an 8 × 8 image and plot all ten. What is each one looking for?

    The question. A bank has to decide who gets a loan. Lending to someone who does not repay costs much more than turning away someone who would have. Can a model make those decisions more cheaply than a simple rule?

    The data. The German Credit dataset: 1,000 loan applicants described by 20 columns, most of them categories. Target: good or bad credit risk.

    from sklearn import datasets
credit = datasets.fetch_openml("credit-g", version=1, as_frame=True)
df = credit.frame

    Baseline. Always predict "good". Then a one-column rule: flag an applicant as "bad" based on their checking account status alone, choosing the rule from the training data.

    Must do. Beat the one-column rule on F1 for "bad", with a range. Then use the cost the dataset comes with: approving a bad applicant costs 5, rejecting a good one costs 1. Choose the probability threshold that gives the lowest total cost on cross-validation, and report the test cost next to the baseline's.

    Watch out for. Thirteen columns are text, so they need encoding, and the encoders and scalers must learn from the training set only. A pipeline makes that automatic. Only 3 in 10 applicants are "bad", so the always-good baseline scores 70 percent accuracy and catches nobody. Two columns, personal_status (sex combined with marital status) and foreign_worker, describe who someone is rather than what they did. Should a credit model use them?

    If you finish early. Drop those two columns, retrain, and measure what it costs in money and in F1. Is the difference larger than your range?

    What you will be asked¶

    Every team gets these questions on presentation day. Prepare the answers in the notebook, not on the morning.

    1. What does your baseline score, and how much did you beat it by, give or take?
    2. How did you split, and how do you know nothing from the test set leaked in?
    3. Show us one case where your model is badly wrong. Why is it wrong there?
    4. What did you try that did not work, and what did it tell you?
    5. If someone used this model tomorrow, what should worry them?

    Before you train anything

    Each team writes down two numbers on paper: what you expect the baseline to score, and what you expect your best model to score. Fold it up and give it to me.

    Most teams overestimate the model and underestimate the baseline. Finding out which way you were wrong is half of what this project is for.
