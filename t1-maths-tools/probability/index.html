    Probability¶

    80 min Foundations Needs: Plotting

    Machine learning is the business of making statements you are not certain about. Probability is the language for saying how uncertain, and statistics is how you check whether the uncertainty you claimed matches reality.

    Why this matters¶

    A classifier does not tell you an image is a cat. It tells you it is 85 percent sure. Every number a model gives you is a claim about probability, and by the end of this programme you will be training models whose entire objective is to make those claims better.

    This page is also where the loss functions of trimester two stop being magic. The thing a neural network minimises is a probability statement, and the last section of this page is that statement.

    The basics, quickly¶

    A probability is a number between 0 and 1. Zero is impossible, one is certain, everything interesting is in between.

    Notation Meaning
    \(P(A)\) the probability that A happens
    \(P(A^c)\) the probability that A does not happen, equal to \(1 - P(A)\)
    \(P(A \cap B)\) both A and B happen
    \(P(A \cup B)\) at least one of A and B happens
    \(P(A \mid B)\) A happens, given that B has happened

    A fair die gives \(P(6) = 1/6\) and \(P(\text{even}) = 1/2\). Nothing here is difficult. The difficulty starts when two events interact.

    Disjoint is not independent¶

    These two words get swapped constantly, and they mean close to opposite things.

    Disjoint means the events cannot both happen. Rolling a 3 and rolling a 5 on one throw.

    Independent means one event tells you nothing about the other. Two separate coin flips.

    If A and B are disjoint and A happens, you now know B did not. That is a very strong piece of information, which is exactly what independence rules out.

    The two rules¶

    Addition, for at least one of them happening:

    \[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

    The subtraction is there because adding the two counts the overlap twice. When the events are disjoint the overlap is zero and the rule simplifies.

    Multiplication, for both of them happening:

    \[ P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A) \]

    When the events are independent, \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\) and this becomes a plain product.

    Drawing two aces from a deck without replacement: the first is \(4/52\), and the second is \(3/51\), because the deck changed. Multiply for about 0.45 percent.

    Conditional probability and Bayes¶

    Conditional probability is the probability of one thing once you know another.

    \[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \]

    Bayes' theorem lets you turn a conditional around, which is the operation almost every classifier performs.

    \[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)} \]

    The formula is easy to apply and hard to feel. So here is the classic example counted out in people rather than symbols.

    A disease affects 1 percent of the population. The test catches 95 percent of people who have it, and wrongly flags 5 percent of people who do not. You test positive. What is the chance you are ill?

    Almost everyone says 95 percent. The answer is about 15.

    The reason is the base rate. There are so many more healthy people that 5 percent of them outnumbers 95 percent of the sick ones, five to one. The test did nothing wrong. The population did it.

    ML connection

    This is class imbalance, which you met on the Working with Data page, seen from the probability side. Your fraud detector will run into exactly this: even a very accurate model, applied to a rare event, produces more false alarms than real catches. When somebody quotes you an accuracy figure, the first question is always what the base rate was.

    You can check the whole thing by simulation, which is often faster than rearranging symbols.

    import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1_000_000
ill = rng.random(n) < 0.01
positive = np.where(ill, rng.random(n) < 0.95, rng.random(n) < 0.05)
print(ill[positive].mean())

    0.16...

    If you can simulate it, you never have to trust your algebra alone.

    Random variables and their distributions¶

    A random variable is a quantity whose value depends on chance. Discrete ones take countable values, such as the number of clicks. Continuous ones take any value in a range, such as a prediction error.

    A distribution describes how probability is spread across the possible values. There are three ways to write one down and they answer different questions.

    The PMF gives the probability of each separate value, and only makes sense for discrete variables. The PDF describes a continuous variable, where probability lives in areas rather than points. The CDF gives the probability of being at or below a value, and it is what percentiles are built from.

    The height of a PDF is not a probability

    It can exceed 1. Only the area under the curve is a probability, which is why \(P(X = x) = 0\) exactly for a continuous variable. Asking for the probability that somebody is exactly 175.000 centimetres tall is asking for the area of a line.

    Describing a distribution with numbers¶

    Expected value is the long run average, weighting each value by how likely it is.

    \[ E[X] = \sum_x x \, P(X = x) \]

    Variance is the average squared distance from that centre, and standard deviation is its square root, which puts it back into the units of the data.

    For the scores 70, 75, 80, 85 and 90, the mean is 80, the variance is 50 and the standard deviation is about 7.07. So a typical score sits about 7 points from the average.

    scores = np.array([70, 75, 80, 85, 90])
print(scores.mean(), scores.var(), scores.std())

    Squaring is what makes variance awkward to interpret and useful to optimise. It is differentiable everywhere and it punishes large errors far more than small ones.

    ML connection

    Mean squared error is the variance of your prediction errors. When you minimise MSE you are shrinking the spread of how wrong you are, and the squaring is why one catastrophic prediction hurts more than ten mediocre ones. That is a choice, not a law, and in trimester two you will meet losses that make a different one.

    Percentiles¶

    The p-th percentile is the value below which p percent of the data falls. The 25th, 50th and 75th are the quartiles, and the gap between the outer two is the interquartile range.

    q1, med, q3 = np.percentile(scores, [25, 50, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
low, high = q1 - 1.5 * iqr, q3 + 1.5 * iqr

    Those last two numbers are the whiskers of the box plot you drew last week, and anything outside them is conventionally called an outlier. Conventionally, not truly. It is a rule of thumb, not a verdict.

    When the mean stops being typical¶

    On a symmetric distribution the mean and median sit together. On a skewed one they separate, and the mean stops describing anybody in particular.

    Report the median for skewed data, and report both when you want to show that the skew exists. A dataset where the mean is far above the median is telling you something before you have plotted anything.

    The distributions worth knowing¶

    Normal¶

    The bell curve. Symmetric, described entirely by a mean \(\mu\) and a standard deviation \(\sigma\).

    The rule worth memorising: 68, 95, 99.7. About 68 percent of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95 percent within two, and 99.7 percent within three. That is how you judge in your head whether a value is unusual.

    heights = rng.normal(loc=170, scale=8, size=10_000)
within_one = np.mean(np.abs(heights - 170) < 8)
print(within_one)

    0.682...

    Bernoulli¶

    One trial with two outcomes, described by a single number \(p\). A coin flip, a click, a fraud or not fraud.

    This is the distribution every binary classifier outputs. When logistic regression returns 0.85, it is handing you a Bernoulli distribution.

    Binomial¶

    The number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials. Mean \(np\), variance \(np(1-p)\).

    successes = rng.binomial(n=10, p=0.5, size=10_000)
print(successes.mean())

    Useful the moment you ask whether a result could have happened by chance. If a model gets 60 out of 100 right and guessing gives 50, the binomial tells you how often luck alone would manage 60.

    Three to recognise¶

    Uniform gives every value in a range the same chance, and is what you get from rng.random(). It is used for random initialisation and for shuffling.

    Poisson counts rare events in a fixed window: emails per hour, arrivals per day. One parameter, \(\lambda\), which is both the mean and the variance.

    Exponential measures the waiting time between those events. If the counts are Poisson, the gaps are exponential.

    You will not use these three often in this programme. Know what they model so you recognise them when they appear.

    Sampling and seeing the shape¶

    The fastest way to understand a distribution is to draw from it and plot it.

    import numpy as np
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(14, 4))
sns.histplot(rng.normal(0, 1, 5000), bins=40, ax=axes[0])
axes[0].set_title("Normal")
sns.histplot(rng.binomial(10, 0.5, 5000), bins=11, ax=axes[1])
axes[1].set_title("Binomial, n=10, p=0.5")
sns.histplot(rng.exponential(1.0, 5000), bins=40, ax=axes[2])
axes[2].set_title("Exponential")
fig.tight_layout()

    Change the parameters and run it again. Ten minutes of this teaches more than reading the formulas twice.

    Why the bell curve is everywhere¶

    The Central Limit Theorem says that if you take averages of samples, those averages tend towards a normal distribution as the sample grows, almost regardless of the shape you started with.

    rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
population = rng.exponential(1.0, 1_000_000)
means_10 = [rng.choice(population, 10).mean() for _ in range(2000)]
means_100 = [rng.choice(population, 100).mean() for _ in range(2000)]
print(np.std(means_10), np.std(means_100))

    The population is strongly skewed and the averages are not. The standard deviation of the averages falls by roughly a factor of three when the sample grows from 10 to 100, because it shrinks with the square root of \(n\).

    Two consequences you will use constantly. Averages are far more trustworthy than single observations. And to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data, which is why collecting more of it has diminishing returns.

    Your test score is an estimate¶

    This is the practical payoff of everything above, and it is the idea that will save you from the most embarrassing mistakes in trimester two.

    When you measure accuracy on a test set, you are measuring a sample. Run the same model on a different sample and you get a different number. The question is whether a difference you observe is real or is noise.

    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
true_accuracy = 0.90
for n in (100, 1000, 10_000):
    trials = rng.binomial(n, true_accuracy, 2000) / n
    print(n, round(trials.min(), 3), round(trials.max(), 3))

    100 0.81 0.98
1000 0.868 0.932
10000 0.891 0.911

    The model never changed. On 100 test rows the measured accuracy wanders between 81 and 98 percent purely by chance. So an improvement from 91 to 92 percent on a small test set is not an improvement, it is weather.

    The rough rule

    The uncertainty on an accuracy measured over \(n\) items is roughly \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). At 90 percent accuracy on 100 items that is about 3 percentage points, so differences smaller than about 6 points mean nothing. Compute it before you celebrate.

    Likelihood, and where loss functions come from¶

    Here is the idea that connects this page to every model you will train.

    A model that outputs probabilities can be scored by asking one question: what probability did it assign to the answer that actually happened? High is good. That number is the likelihood.

    Multiply those probabilities across thousands of examples and you get a number so small that a computer cannot represent it. So we take logarithms, which turn products into sums, and then flip the sign so that smaller is better.

    \[ \text{loss} = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_i \log p_i \]

    where \(p_i\) is the probability the model gave to the correct answer for example \(i\). That is cross-entropy, and it is the loss function behind almost every classifier you will build.

    The shape of the negative logarithm is the whole story. Give the right answer 90 percent and the cost is 0.105. Give it 5 percent and the cost is 3.0, thirty times more. As the probability approaches zero the cost grows without limit.

    So the function does not merely punish being wrong. It punishes being confident and wrong, and it rewards honest uncertainty. A model that says 60 percent and is occasionally wrong scores better than one that says 99 percent and is occasionally wrong.

    p_correct = np.array([0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 0.05])
print(-np.log(p_correct))
print(-np.log(p_correct).mean())

    [0.105 0.223 0.511 2.996]
0.959

    One bad prediction dominates the average. That single line explains most of what you will see when you start watching training curves.

    Correlation, and its limits¶

    Covariance measures whether two variables move together. Correlation is covariance rescaled to sit between -1 and 1, which makes it comparable across different units.

    x = rng.normal(0, 1, 1000)
y = 2 * x + rng.normal(0, 1, 1000)
print(np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1])

    0.89...

    Two warnings, both of which will cost somebody marks this trimester.

    Correlation only measures straight line relationships. A perfect U shape can have a correlation near zero. This is why you plot before you trust the number, which is the same lesson as Anscombe's quartet from the Plotting page.

    Correlation is not causation. Ice cream sales correlate with drownings. Both are caused by summer. A model does not care, because a correlated feature predicts perfectly well until the underlying cause shifts and the relationship quietly stops holding.

    Common confusions¶

    Disjoint and independent

    Disjoint means they cannot both happen. Independent means one tells you nothing about the other. Disjoint events with non-zero probability are always dependent.

    \(P(A \mid B)\) and \(P(B \mid A)\)

    These are not the same number and are often wildly different. The probability of testing positive given illness was 95 percent. The probability of illness given a positive test was 15. Swapping these two is the single most common probability error in the wild.

    The PDF height looks like a probability

    It is not, and it can exceed 1. Probability is the area under the curve between two values.

    A big correlation means one causes the other

    It means they move together. It says nothing at all about why.

    My model improved from 91 to 92 percent

    On how many test items? Work out the uncertainty first. On a few hundred rows that difference is indistinguishable from noise.

    Dividing by n or by n minus 1

    Use \(n\) when your data is the whole population, and \(n-1\) when it is a sample you are using to estimate a wider population. NumPy defaults to \(n\), and pandas defaults to \(n-1\), which is why the two sometimes disagree by a little. For large datasets the difference is negligible.

    Check yourself¶

    Use NumPy rather than algebra wherever you can. Simulating is a legitimate way to answer these.

    1. Simulate 100,000 rolls of two dice. Estimate the probability that the sum is 7, and compare it with the exact answer.
    2. Change the disease prevalence in the medical test simulation from 1 percent to 30 percent. Recompute the chance of being ill given a positive test, and explain in one sentence why it moved so much.
    3. Draw 10,000 values from a normal distribution of your choice and verify the 68, 95, 99.7 rule yourself.
    4. Take an exponential population, then plot the distribution of the mean of 5 samples and of 50 samples. Describe what changes.
    5. A model is 85 percent accurate. How large must your test set be before a 2 percentage point improvement is distinguishable from noise? Answer by simulation.
    6. Compute the cross-entropy loss for a model that gives the right answer 0.99, 0.99, 0.99 and 0.01, and for one that gives 0.7 four times. Which is the better model, and does the number agree with your instinct?

    Quick reference¶

    Idea Formula or code
    Complement \(P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)\)
    Addition \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\)
    Multiplication \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B \mid A)\)
    Independence \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)\)
    Conditional \(P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B) / P(B)\)
    Bayes \(P(A \mid B) = P(B \mid A) P(A) / P(B)\)
    Mean, variance, sd a.mean(), a.var(), a.std()
    Percentiles np.percentile(a, [25, 50, 75])
    Correlation np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]
    Seeded generator rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    Normal sample rng.normal(loc, scale, size)
    Binomial sample rng.binomial(n, p, size)
    Uniform sample rng.random(size)
    Cross-entropy -np.log(p_correct).mean()
    Accuracy uncertainty \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\)

    Questions to sit with¶

    1. The test in the Bayes example was 95 percent accurate and a positive result still meant an 85 percent chance of being healthy. If you were designing a national screening programme, what would you do with that fact?
    2. Cross-entropy punishes confident wrong answers far more than uncertain ones. What behaviour does that encourage in a model, and can you think of a situation where you would not want it?
    3. Minimising mean squared error means treating one error of size 10 as worse than ten errors of size 1. Name a problem where that is right, and one where it is badly wrong.
    4. To halve your uncertainty you need four times the data. What does that imply about projects that promise big gains from collecting a bit more?
    5. A feature correlates strongly with your target and you have no idea why. Do you use it? Does your answer change if the model decides who gets a loan?
