    Classification¶

    70 min Classic models The map for this group

    This page teaches no algorithm. It is the map you will read the next four projects against, so that each one feels like filling in a region rather than arriving somewhere unfamiliar.

    Why this matters¶

    You have spent a trimester producing a clean, encoded, split table. A model is what finally reads it.

    Newcomers assume that this is where the skill lives, and that choosing the right algorithm is the job. It is not. Switching between reasonable models usually moves your score by a couple of points. What moves it far more is the work you already did, and what protects it is knowing how to tell a real improvement from a flattering one.

    So this page covers four things: what kind of question you are asking, what kinds of model exist, how they all share one interface, and how you will know whether any of them worked.

    Where models sit¶

    Notice the two loops. The short one sends you back to try another model or another setting. The long one sends you back to the data.

    Beginners spend most of their time on the short loop. People who have done this for a while spend most of theirs on the long one, because that is where the gains are.

    Classification and regression¶

    Every supervised problem is one of these two, and the difference is what the answer looks like.

    Classification predicts a category. Will this customer churn, is this email spam, which of ten digits is this.

    Regression predicts a number. How much will this flat sell for, how many units will we ship next month.

    The same features can serve either. From the churn data you could predict whether somebody leaves, which is classification, or how many months they will stay, which is regression. The question you ask determines the model family, the metric and the loss function, so it is worth being explicit about it before you start.

    Classification comes in three shapes¶

    Shape One example gets Example
    Binary One of two labels Churn or stay
    Multi-class One of many labels Which of three internet plans
    Multi-label Any number of labels at once Which services a customer holds

    You met these on the Framing a Problem page. The distinction matters here because it changes which metrics are available to you and how the model's output is shaped.

    A classifier gives you a probability, not an answer¶

    This is the part that surprises people, and it is the most useful thing in this section.

    model.predict(X_test)
model.predict_proba(X_test)

    ['No' 'Yes' 'No']
[[0.82 0.18]
 [0.31 0.69]
 [0.95 0.05]]

    The model produces a probability for each class. predict then applies a threshold, by default 0.5, and hands you a label.

    That threshold is a choice, and it is yours. Lower it to 0.3 and you catch more churners and raise more false alarms. Raise it to 0.7 and the opposite. Nothing about the model changed. You moved a dial that trades one kind of mistake against the other.

    ML connection

    The probability comes from the cross-entropy loss you met on the Probability page. The model is trained to make the probability it assigns to the correct answer as high as possible, which is why predict_proba exists at all.

    Where to put the threshold is a business decision. If missing a churner costs you a customer and a false alarm costs you a phone call, those are not equally bad and 0.5 is almost certainly the wrong place.

    The families of algorithms¶

    Each of these gets its own project. What follows is what they are, so the map is complete before you walk into the first one.

    Linear models. Logistic regression for classification, linear regression for numbers. They assume the effect of each feature adds up. Fast, and the coefficients tell you what the model thinks, which is why they are still the default in medicine and finance. Need scaling.

    Tree-based models. A decision tree asks a series of yes or no questions. One tree is easy to read and unstable. Many trees combined, as a random forest or a gradient boosted ensemble, are the strongest thing you will use on tabular data all year. No scaling needed, handles mixed types, less interpretable as the ensemble grows.

    Distance-based models. K-nearest neighbours answers by looking at the most similar rows it has seen. No training as such, all the work happens at prediction time. Scaling is critical here, because the distance is meaningless without it.

    Probabilistic models. Naive Bayes applies Bayes' theorem while assuming the features are independent. That assumption is almost never true and the model often works anyway. Very fast, still competitive for text.

    Margin-based models. Support vector machines find the boundary with the widest gap between classes, and can bend it into curves. Strong on small and medium datasets, slow on large ones, and the results are hard to explain.

    Family Scaling Categoricals Interpretable Good first choice for
    Linear Required Encode first Very Anything you must explain
    Trees, single No Encode first Very Understanding the data
    Trees, ensemble No Encode first Somewhat Tabular data, usually the winner
    Distance based Critical Encode first Poorly Small clean datasets
    Probabilistic No Naturally Somewhat Text, and speed
    Margin based Required Encode first Poorly Small, hard boundaries

    Unsupervised learning is on this map too¶

    Where clustering fits

    Everything above is supervised: you have labels and the model learns to reproduce them.

    Two of the projects in this group have no labels at all. Unsupervised Learning and Clustering ask a different question: given these rows and no answers, what natural groups exist? There is no target column, so none of the metrics on this page apply, and success is judged differently.

    It is the same map, a different continent. Keep it in view, because when somebody hands you data with no labels, the supervised toolkit is not an option.

    What the families actually differ on¶

    One picture explains more than the table above.

    Every model family can only draw certain shapes. That constraint is the assumption it makes about the world, fixed before it ever saw your data.

    A linear model assumes one straight cut separates the classes. A tree assumes the boundary can be built from rectangles aligned to the axes, which is why trees cope so well with a feature that only matters above a threshold. A distance-based model assumes similar rows have similar answers, and its boundary bends around individual points.

    When a model performs badly, this is the first thing to ask: is the boundary it needs a shape this family can draw?

    One interface for all of them¶

    The practical consequence of everything above is small and liberating.

    from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

models = {
    "logistic": LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000),
    "forest": RandomForestClassifier(random_state=42),
    "knn": KNeighborsClassifier(),
}
for name, model in models.items():
    model.fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(name, model.score(X_test, y_test))

    Four methods cover almost everything: fit to learn, predict for labels, predict_proba for probabilities, score for a quick number.

    Swapping a model is one line. That is why the pipeline you built on the last page matters so much: with the preprocessing fixed inside it, every model sees identically prepared data and the comparison is fair.

    model = Pipeline([("prep", preprocess), ("clf", RandomForestClassifier())])

    Change the final step and nothing else moves.

    Start with something stupid¶

    Before any real model, build one that cannot possibly be good.

    from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier

dummy = DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent")
dummy.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(dummy.score(X_test, y_test))

    0.735

    On the churn data, predicting "nobody leaves" is 73.5 percent accurate. That is your baseline, and it is the number every result must be compared against.

    A model at 76 percent accuracy sounds respectable and is barely better than answering the same thing every time. Without the baseline on the page you would not know that, and neither would the person reading your report.

    Simple first, complicated only if it earns it

    Every step up in complexity costs you: longer training, harder explanations, more hyperparameters, and more opportunities to fool yourself.

    A logistic regression that the person acting on it can understand often beats a better model they will not trust. Reach for something heavier when the simpler thing has been tried and found wanting, not before.

    Too simple, too complex¶

    Underfitting means the model is too simple to represent the pattern. It is wrong on the training data as well as on new data, which makes it easy to spot.

    Overfitting means the model has memorised the training data, including its noise. Perfect where it trained and poor everywhere else.

    The gap between the two scores is the diagnosis:

    Training score Test score What it means
    Low Low Underfitting. More capacity, or better features
    High High You are done
    High Low Overfitting. Simpler model, more data, or regularisation
    Low High Something is wrong with your split

    This is the same picture as the training and validation curves from the Fundamentals page, now with a model attached instead of a neural network. It is also why the held back set exists at all: without it, the third row and the second row look identical.

    How you will be judged¶

    Accuracy is the number everyone quotes and the one that hides most.

    On the churn data, 73.5 percent accuracy is achievable by a model that never predicts churn. Any serious evaluation starts with the full picture.

    Precision answers: of everyone the model flagged, how many really were churners? It is the column. Low precision means wasted effort on false alarms.

    Recall answers: of everyone who really churned, how many did the model find? It is the row. Low recall means customers walking out unnoticed.

    The two trade against each other, and the dial is the threshold from earlier. Lower it and recall rises while precision falls.

    F1 combines them into one number, the harmonic mean, which stays low unless both are decent. It is the usual single figure to quote when the classes are imbalanced.

    from sklearn.metrics import classification_report, confusion_matrix

print(confusion_matrix(y_test, model.predict(X_test)))
print(classification_report(y_test, model.predict(X_test)))

    ROC AUC measures how well the model ranks, across every possible threshold at once. It is useful for comparing models before you have chosen a threshold, and it can look flattering on very imbalanced data.

    For regression the equivalents are MAE, the average size of the error in the original units, and RMSE, which punishes large errors more because of the squaring you met in Probability.

    Choose the metric before you see the scores

    If you pick the metric afterwards, you will pick the one that makes your model look best. Write down which number matters, and why, while you are still framing the problem.

    For churn, ask what the company does with a prediction. If they phone the customer, false alarms are cheap and recall matters. If they send a three month discount, false alarms are expensive and precision matters.

    Questions to sit with¶

    1. A classifier outputs a probability and a threshold turns it into a decision. Who in an organisation should choose that threshold, and what would they need to know to choose it well?
    2. Your model scores 76 percent where the baseline is 73.5. Is that a result? What would you need to say alongside the number for it to be honest?
    3. Every model family can only draw certain shapes of boundary. What does that mean for the claim that a model learns from data with no assumptions?
    4. Precision and recall trade against each other. Describe a problem where you would accept terrible precision for high recall, and one where you would do the reverse.
    5. The long loop in the first diagram goes back to the data. Why do beginners prefer the short one, and what does that preference cost them?
