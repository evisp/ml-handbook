    Linear Models¶

    85 min Classic models Needs: Classification

    A linear model adds things up. That is the whole idea, and it is why these models are still the default wherever somebody has to explain the answer to a regulator, a doctor or a customer.

    Why this matters¶

    Linear models are where you learn to read a model rather than only run one. The coefficients are the model: there is nothing else inside. That makes them the right place to meet regularisation, feature selection and explainability, because you can check what every technique did by looking.

    They are also the floor. On tabular data a linear model is often within a few points of a much heavier one, and when it is not, the gap tells you something about your problem.

    What a linear model is¶

    \[ \hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_n x_n + b \]

    Each feature gets a weight. Multiply, add, done. The weights \(w\) are called coefficients and \(b\) is the intercept.

    Training means choosing the coefficients that make the predictions as close as possible to the truth, where "close" means the mean squared error you met in Probability.

    ML connection

    You have seen this picture before, as a single unit on the What Training Actually Does page. A linear model is one of those units, and a neural network is many of them stacked with a squashing function in between.

    Understanding this one properly is not preparation for the real thing. It is the real thing, at its smallest.

    from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

model = LinearRegression()
model.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(model.intercept_)
for name, coef in zip(X_train.columns, model.coef_):
    print(f"{name:25s} {coef:+.3f}")

    Read a coefficient like this: holding everything else constant, one extra unit of this feature changes the prediction by this much. A coefficient of \(-1.6\) on tenure means each extra month of tenure lowers the prediction by 1.6.

    Coefficients are only comparable if the features are scaled

    A coefficient of 0.53 on monthly charges and 31 on a yes or no flag does not mean the flag matters sixty times more. They are measured in different units.

    Scale the features first, as you did on the Data Preparation page, and the coefficients become comparable. For regularised models it is not optional at all, for reasons in the next section.

    Judging a regression¶

    Classification gave you accuracy, precision and recall. Regression predicts a number, so the question changes to how far off you were.

    MAE, mean absolute error, is the average size of the error, in the original units. Easy to explain: "on average we are 47 euro out".

    MSE, mean squared error, squares each error before averaging. Harder to read, because the units are squared, and it is what the model actually minimises.

    RMSE is the square root of MSE, which puts it back into the original units while keeping the squaring's effect: large errors count for much more than small ones.

    R squared answers a different question: what fraction of the variation in the target does the model explain? 1.0 is perfect, 0.0 is no better than always predicting the mean, and negative is worse than that.

    import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error, r2_score

pred = model.predict(X_test)
print("MAE ", mean_absolute_error(y_test, pred))
print("RMSE", np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_test, pred)))
print("R2  ", r2_score(y_test, pred))

    Which to report: MAE when every unit of error costs the same, RMSE when one large mistake is worse than several small ones, and R squared alongside either, because it is the only one that is comparable across different problems.

    An RMSE of 47 means nothing on its own. An RMSE of 47 where the target ranges from 20 to 8000 is excellent, and where it ranges from 0 to 100 is terrible.

    Regularisation¶

    The Classification page showed you overfitting. This is the fix.

    An unconstrained linear model will happily give a feature an enormous coefficient if that squeezes a little more accuracy out of the training data. Large coefficients mean the prediction swings wildly on small changes in input, which is overfitting in its most readable form.

    Regularisation adds a penalty for large coefficients, so the model has to trade fit against simplicity.

    \[ \text{minimise} \quad \text{error} + \alpha \times \text{penalty on the coefficients} \]

    Alpha controls the trade. Zero gives you plain linear regression. Very large drives every coefficient towards zero and the model predicts the mean.

    The two common penalties behave very differently, and the reason is geometric.

    Ridge penalises the squared coefficients. Its constraint region is round, so the solution almost never lands exactly on an axis. Everything shrinks, nothing disappears.

    Lasso penalises the absolute values. Its region is a diamond with corners on the axes, and corners are exactly where the error contours tend to touch first. When that happens a coefficient becomes exactly zero.

    from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge, Lasso
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

ridge = Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()), ("model", Ridge(alpha=1.0))])
lasso = Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()), ("model", Lasso(alpha=0.1))])
ridge.fit(X_train, y_train)
lasso.fit(X_train, y_train)
kept = (lasso.named_steps["model"].coef_ != 0).sum()
print(f"lasso kept {kept} of {X_train.shape[1]} features")

    Use Ridge when you believe most features contribute something, and when several are correlated. Ridge spreads the weight across a correlated group rather than picking one arbitrarily.

    Use Lasso when you suspect most features are useless and you want the model to tell you which. The surviving coefficients are a feature selection.

    Use ElasticNet when you want both, which is a mix of the two penalties.

    Scaling is mandatory here, not advisory

    The penalty is applied to the coefficients. A feature measured in thousands gets a tiny coefficient and is barely penalised. A feature measured in single digits gets a large one and is punished hard.

    Without scaling, alpha means something different for every column and the model's choices are driven by your units rather than by your data. Put the scaler in the pipeline and this cannot happen.

    Choose alpha with cross-validation rather than by eye.

    from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV

ridge_cv = RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-3, 3, 13))

    Explaining a prediction¶

    Coefficients tell you what the model does in general. They do not tell you why it said what it said about this customer.

    SHAP answers that. For a single prediction it splits the difference between the model's output and its average output into one contribution per feature.

    That is precisely the diagram at the top of this page: value times coefficient, one bar per feature. For a linear model SHAP reproduces it exactly. The reason SHAP is worth learning is that it produces the same picture for a random forest, a gradient boosted tree or a neural network, where no coefficients exist.

    import shap

explainer = shap.Explainer(model, X_train)
shap_values = explainer(X_test)
shap.plots.waterfall(shap_values[0])
shap.plots.beeswarm(shap_values)

    Two plots, two questions.

    Waterfall explains one prediction. It starts at the average and adds each feature's push up or down until it reaches this customer's answer. This is what you show to the person who has to act on the prediction.

    Beeswarm explains the model. Every point is one customer, features are ordered by importance, and the colour shows whether the feature value was high or low. It tells you not just which features matter but in which direction.

    An explanation is not a cause

    SHAP tells you what the model used. It does not tell you what is true about the world.

    If the model leans on a feature that happens to correlate with the outcome, SHAP will faithfully report that, and somebody will read it as a finding about customers rather than a finding about the model. This is the correlation and causation problem from Probability, arriving in a more persuasive costume.

    From regression to classification¶

    Logistic regression is the same linear model with one extra step.

    The linear part produces a score that can be any number. The sigmoid squashes it into the range 0 to 1, which makes it a probability.

    \[ p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(w \cdot x + b)}} \]
    from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

clf = Pipeline([
    ("scale", StandardScaler()),
    ("model", LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
])
clf.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(clf.predict_proba(X_test)[:3])

    Despite the name it is a classifier, not a regressor. The confusing name is historical and you will have to live with it.

    The coefficients still mean something, just not the thing they mean in linear regression. A logistic coefficient is a change in the log odds. Exponentiate it and you get an odds ratio, which is how it is reported in medicine.

    import numpy as np

odds_ratio = np.exp(clf.named_steps["model"].coef_[0])

    An odds ratio of 1.8 on a feature means the odds of the positive class are 1.8 times higher when that feature increases by one unit, holding the rest constant.

    The decision boundary¶

    Where the probability equals 0.5, the linear score is zero. That set of points is a straight line in two dimensions, a plane in three, and a flat surface in more.

    This is what the Classification page drew as the linear family's boundary. It is a hard constraint: if your classes cannot be separated by a straight cut, no amount of tuning a logistic regression will fix it, and you need either better features or a different family.

    Regularisation applies here too, through the C parameter, which is the inverse of alpha. Small C means strong regularisation.

    Support vector machines¶

    An SVM asks a sharper question. Many lines separate two groups. Which one should you choose?

    The answer is the one with the widest gap, called the margin. The points sitting on the edge of that gap are the support vectors, and they are the only ones that matter. Move any other point and the boundary does not change.

    C controls how much you are willing to bend. A large C insists on classifying every training point correctly, giving a narrow margin and probably overfitting. A small C accepts some mistakes in exchange for a wider, more robust margin.

    Kernels¶

    A straight boundary is often not enough. Rather than adding polynomial features by hand, an SVM can use a kernel, which computes what the answer would be in a higher dimensional space without ever building it.

    from sklearn.svm import SVC

for kernel in ("linear", "poly", "rbf"):
    svm = Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()), ("model", SVC(kernel=kernel))])
    svm.fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(kernel, svm.score(X_test, y_test))
    Kernel Boundary Try it when
    linear A straight cut Many features, or you want speed and interpretability
    poly A smooth curve You expect interactions between features
    rbf Closed regions around clusters The classes are tangled and you have the data to support it

    rbf is the usual default and has a second parameter, gamma, which controls how far the influence of one point reaches. High gamma means each point only affects its immediate neighbourhood, which produces a wiggly boundary that overfits. Low gamma approaches a straight line.

    So C and gamma both control complexity, and they interact. Tune them together with a grid search rather than one at a time.

    SVMs need scaling and do not scale up

    The margin is measured as a distance, so an unscaled feature with a large range dominates it entirely. Always scale.

    Training time grows roughly with the square of the number of rows, so an SVM on a hundred thousand rows is painful and on a million is impractical. On the churn data it is fine. On something larger, reach for a linear model or a tree ensemble.

    Evaluating the classifiers¶

    Everything from the Classification page applies unchanged: confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1, ROC AUC, and choosing the metric before you see the scores.

    from sklearn.metrics import classification_report, roc_auc_score

print(classification_report(y_test, clf.predict(X_test)))
print(roc_auc_score(y_test, clf.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]))

    One addition specific to this page. SVC does not give you probabilities by default, because it produces distances from the boundary rather than probabilities. Pass probability=True if you need them, and expect training to take noticeably longer.

    Compare every model against the dummy baseline, and compare them with cross-validation rather than a single split, so that you are not reading noise.

    from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

for name, m in [("logistic", clf), ("svm", svm), ("ridge", ridge)]:
    scores = cross_val_score(m, X_train, y_train, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc")
    print(f"{name:10s} {scores.mean():.3f} plus or minus {scores.std():.3f}")

    Print the spread as well as the mean. A model that scores 0.81 give or take 0.01 is better than one that scores 0.82 give or take 0.05, and the second number is the one everybody forgets.

    When it goes wrong¶

    ConvergenceWarning: lbfgs failed to converge

    Usually unscaled features. Put a StandardScaler in the pipeline. If it persists, raise max_iter.

    Lasso set every coefficient to zero

    Alpha is too large, so the model found that predicting the mean was cheaper than using any feature. Try a much smaller alpha, on a log scale.

    Ridge and plain linear regression give identical results

    Alpha is effectively zero relative to the size of your errors, or your features are unscaled so the penalty is not biting. Check both.

    The coefficients change completely when I refit

    Two or more features are highly correlated, so the model can distribute the weight between them in many equivalent ways. Ridge stabilises this. It is also a sign to check the correlation heatmap you drew during preparation.

    SVM training never finishes

    Too many rows, or C is very large, or gamma is very high. Subsample first to find workable parameters, then scale up.

    SHAP is extremely slow

    You are using the general explainer on a large background set. Pass a sample of a hundred rows as the background, and use the linear or tree explainer when your model is one of those.

    Check yourself¶

    Use the churn data, or any dataset with a numeric target for the regression parts.

    1. Fit a linear regression, print the coefficients, and write one sentence interpreting the largest one in plain language.
    2. Report MAE, RMSE and R squared on the same predictions. Explain which you would put in a report and why.
    3. Fit Ridge across a range of alphas and plot how one coefficient changes. Describe the shape.
    4. Fit Lasso and report how many features survived. Name two that were dropped and say whether you agree.
    5. Deliberately remove the scaler from a Ridge pipeline and compare the coefficients. Explain the difference.
    6. Produce a SHAP waterfall for one customer and write the explanation you would give to that customer.
    7. Fit a logistic regression, convert one coefficient to an odds ratio, and state what it means.
    8. Compare linear, poly and rbf kernels with cross-validation, reporting the mean and the spread. Say which you would ship and why.

    Questions to sit with¶

    1. A coefficient says the prediction changes by this much when the feature changes by one, holding everything else constant. When is "holding everything else constant" impossible in the real world, and what does that do to your interpretation?
    2. Lasso drops features for you. What could it drop that you would want to keep, and how would you notice?
    3. SHAP explains what the model used. A customer asks why they were refused. Is a SHAP waterfall an adequate answer? Would it satisfy a regulator?
    4. A linear model gets 0.80 and an RBF kernel gets 0.82. Which do you ship, and what else would you want to know before deciding?
    5. Regularisation deliberately makes the fit on training data worse. Explain to somebody non-technical why that is the point.
